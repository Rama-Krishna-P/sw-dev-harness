# The Ghost in the Break Room

At 11:47 on a Tuesday night, the vending machine in the break room began whispering.

Mara Bell was the only person on the ninth floor of Harker & Vale, unless one counted the cardboard cutout of the company founder, which had been standing beside the elevators since 2018 and had never once offered to help with a deadline.

“Please select a snack,” whispered the machine.

Mara froze with one hand inside the communal refrigerator. “I haven’t selected anything.”

“Exactly.”

She slowly withdrew her hand. The refrigerator light flickered. Somewhere beyond the glass walls, the city glittered with the careless confidence of people who had gone home on time.

Mara looked at the vending machine. It was the old blue one, the kind that sold chips, candy bars, and a rotating selection of items no one could identify. Its screen showed a single message:

**EMPLOYEE DISCOUNT: ONE SOUL FOR THE PRICE OF TWO SNACKS.**

“That’s not an approved promotion,” Mara said.

“Neither is your overtime.”

Mara reached for her phone. No signal. Of course. Harker & Vale’s ninth floor had dead zones, draft zones, and one zone where the printer could sense fear. She tried the emergency number anyway.

The vending machine hummed.

“Don’t call Security,” it said. “They’re already here.”

The break-room door swung open.

Behind it stood Len from Accounts Payable, wearing slippers, a bathrobe, and the expression of a man who had just been informed that his expense report required “more context.”

“Mara,” he said. “Did you hear that?”

“The machine?”

“No, the screaming.”

From inside the vending machine came a muffled cry: “I regret choosing the family-size pretzels!”

Len adjusted his glasses. “That’s new.”

Mara had worked at Harker & Vale for six years. She had survived three reorganizations, two mergers, and a team-building exercise involving blindfolds and a trust fall onto a beanbag chair. She had learned not to panic until someone used the phrase “exciting opportunity.”

“We need to get out,” she said.

They ran for the elevators. The doors opened before Mara pressed the button.

Inside, every floor button was glowing red except the ninth. A handwritten note had been taped above the panel.

**PLEASE ENJOY THE JOURNEY TO THE BASEMENT.**

“I’m not going to the basement,” Mara said.

The elevator chimed.

“The elevator disagrees,” said Len.

The doors closed. As they descended, the lights dimmed and a soft piano melody drifted from the ceiling speaker. Mara recognized it as the company’s hold music.

“This is how horror movies begin,” Len whispered.

“No. Horror movies begin with people making bad decisions.”

The elevator stopped between floors.

The speaker crackled. A woman’s voice said, “Welcome to your mandatory after-hours engagement survey.”

Len pressed the emergency button.

“Question one,” said the voice. “On a scale of one to ten, how likely are you to recommend being alive to a colleague?”

“Zero,” Len said.

“Thank you for your honesty.”

The elevator lurched down. Mara braced herself against the wall. The cardboard founder’s face appeared on the floor indicator, smiling with the vacant warmth of a man who had never had to reset a password.

“Question two,” he said. “What is one thing Harker & Vale could do to improve your experience?”

“Close,” Mara answered.

The elevator reached the basement.

The doors opened onto a conference room that had not existed yesterday.

Long tables stretched beneath fluorescent lights. Around them sat dozens of translucent office workers in outdated business clothes. Some had spectral name badges. One wore a tie with tiny pictures of fax machines.

At the head of the room stood a pale woman holding a clipboard.

“Finally,” she said. “The living have arrived.”

Len raised a hand. “Are you Human Resources?”

“Worse. I’m the former office manager.”

The ghosts turned toward Mara and Len. Their faces were gray, their eyes hollow, and their expressions unmistakably managerial.

The woman tapped the clipboard. “We are the Night Shift. Employees who died before completing their performance reviews.”

“How did you die?” Mara asked.

“A printer fire,” said the woman.

“A printer fire?”

“The printer jammed. We all gathered to help. It sensed weakness.”

Len swallowed. “What do you want?”

The woman pointed toward a projector screen. A presentation appeared: **GHOSTS: ALIGNING EXPECTATIONS FOR A BETTER AFTERLIFE.**

“We want recognition,” she said. “For thirty years, the company has ignored our unfinished work. We want the executive floor to acknowledge that we exist.”

Mara stared at the presentation. The next slide read: **ACTION ITEM: POSSESS CEO.**

“You could haunt the CEO,” she said.

“We tried. He works remotely.”

“Send him an email.”

The ghosts murmured. The office manager looked offended. “We are dead, not desperate.”

The vending machine rolled into the conference room on its own little wheels. Its blue panels bulged as if something inside were trying to breathe.

“The machine is taking souls,” Mara said.

“It is our benefits administrator,” said the office manager. “We needed a living employee to complete the enrollment process.”

“You kidnapped people for paperwork?”

“We prefer ‘temporarily retained.’”

Mara looked at the trapped pretzel-eater, then at Len. Len had gone very still.

“What?” she asked.

He pointed to a spreadsheet projected on the wall. “That’s my department’s reconciliation report.”

Rows of names filled the screen. Beside each name was a status: **PENDING, PENDING, PENDING.** At the bottom, in red letters, was **LEN: SOON.**

“I knew I should have taken that job at the aquarium,” Len said.

Mara’s fear hardened into the same grim focus that had carried her through year-end close. She walked to the projector and studied the ghostly presentation.

“You don’t need a soul,” she said. “You need an escalation path.”

The ghosts leaned closer.

“Every company has one,” Mara continued. “If the executive floor won’t acknowledge you, we create an incident they can’t ignore.”

The office manager narrowed her eyes. “What kind of incident?”

“A compliance incident.”

The ghosts gasped. One fainted directly through a chair.

Mara explained the plan. The ghosts would possess every office appliance at midnight. The printers would release thirty years of unprocessed complaints. The conference-room screens would display the names of the dead. The elevators would refuse to stop at any floor unless someone said their names aloud.

“And the vending machine?” asked Len.

“It will refund everyone.”

The machine made a grinding noise.

“I don’t do refunds.”

“Then you’re not a vending machine,” Mara said. “You’re a hostile snack-based financial system.”

The machine considered this. Its display changed.

**FINE. ONE REFUND. NO QUESTIONS.**

At midnight, the haunting began.

On the executive floor, the CEO was awakened by his laptop opening itself. His screen filled with a list of names and a single subject line: **RE: YOUR UNRESOLVED PEOPLE PROBLEM.**

In the lobby, the cardboard founder turned his head and asked the night guard for a raise.

Printers all over the building began producing paper. Not reports, not invoices, but handwritten grievances.

**THE BREAK ROOM COFFEE HAS BEEN DECAF SINCE 2009.**

**WHO STOLE MY YOGURT? I KNOW YOU ARE DEAD, CARL.**

**THE OFFICE PLANT WAS NEVER WATERED.**

The elevators opened on every floor, and a calm voice announced the names of the Night Shift. By 12:15, the CEO had called an emergency meeting. By 12:20, he had called a priest. By 12:25, the priest had asked whether the company offered dental.

At 12:31, the trapped employees were released from the vending machine in a shower of candy wrappers. The family-size pretzel man landed on the carpet and immediately asked whether he was still eligible for benefits.

The executive floor doors opened. The CEO stood there in pajamas, flanked by two vice presidents and a priest who had been given a visitor badge.

The office manager floated forward. “We want acknowledgment.”

The CEO looked at the ghosts. Then he looked at Mara.

“Who is responsible for this?”

Mara pointed at the cardboard founder.

The founder pointed at the vending machine.

The vending machine displayed **NO COMMENT**.

The CEO sighed. “Fine. We’ll create a memorial plaque.”

“And complete the reviews,” said the office manager.

“Fine.”

“And restore the coffee budget.”

“Fine.”

“And approve remote work for the dead.”

The CEO blinked. “That seems unnecessary.”

Every printer in the building started screaming.

“Fine!” he shouted.

The ghosts smiled. One by one, they faded into the fluorescent air, leaving behind a soft scent of toner and unresolved resentment.

By morning, Harker & Vale had a new memorial wall, a coffee budget, and a policy recognizing spectral employees as “legacy contributors.” The vending machine was moved to the lobby, where it charged tourists double.

Mara received a promotion for demonstrating “exceptional crisis leadership.” It came with a larger workload and no salary increase.

Len accepted the aquarium job.

On her last night, Mara returned to the break room to collect her mug. The lights flickered. The vending machine hummed.

“You did well,” it said.

“Thanks.”

“Would you like a snack?”

Mara looked at the display. It now read:

**EMPLOYEE DISCOUNT: ONE SOUL FOR THE PRICE OF TWO SNACKS.**

“No,” she said.

The machine’s coils turned. A granola bar dropped into the tray.

“On the house,” it whispered. “For surviving management.”

Mara picked up the bar. The wrapper was cold.

On the back, beneath the nutrition facts, someone had printed a new warning:

**MAY CONTAIN TRACES OF GHOST.**

Mara smiled, put the bar in her bag, and went home before anything else could schedule a meeting.
